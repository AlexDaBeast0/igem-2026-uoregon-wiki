# Our Guiding Question

Synthetic biology bridges biology, engineering, and creative problem-solving. Because of both the breadth and depth of expertise that the field encompasses, we tailored our educational curricula to meet the needs and perspectives of students with diverse knowledge levels and academic interests.

For our education initiatives, we designed three original curriculums tailored to three unique audiences, creating opportunities for students with varying prior knowledge about synthetic biology to meaningfully engage with this interdisciplinary field.

Our education programs were therefore developed around the central question:

{% include box.html
  text="How can we make synthetic biology accessible and engaging for diverse student audiences?"
%}

Rather than developing one generic curriculum and delivering it to every audience, we approached education both as an iterative process and as a conversation between us as educators and the students as learners. We modeled our program development after a DBTL (Design, Build, Test, Learn) framework, modeling our workshops based on the post-event experiences of our student audiences.

Across three iterations, we developed workshops for audiences with increasing amounts of prior knowledge.

<br>

**Workshop 1:** **Intro to Synthetic Biology**

**Audience:** 30 high school students with minimal science background and diverse career interests

**Format:** 1-hour workshop + worksheet

<br>

**Workshop 2: Engineering SynBio Solutions**

**Audience:** 20 high school students with minimal science background who are interested in science

**Format:** 2-hour workshop + fully illustrated workbook + comic

<br>

**Workshop 3: SynBio Solutions to Real-World Problems**

**Audience:** 20 undergraduate science students

**Format:** 2-hour workshop + illustrated workbook + hands-on activities

<br>

Each workshop built on what we learned from the previous event, resulting in an informed process of improving our curriculum as we tailor it to our audiences.

Our goal is not to remove the complexity of synthetic biology as we design curricula for our student audiences. Instead, we aim to create pathways that allow students with different levels of prior knowledge and interests to engage with this complexity. We accomplish this by introducing content in a manner that naturally builds on broader knowledge to guide students to higher level thinking when more complex concepts are introduced.

# The Challenge

High school and early-undergraduate science education is often organized around established disciplines such as biology, chemistry, physics, and mathematics. While these subjects are widely represented in U.S. public schools, opportunities to engage with more specialized and interdisciplinary areas of STEM are less consistently available (CITE).

This presents a particular challenge for synthetic biology and bioengineering as these fields draw on concepts from multiple disciplines such as engineering, bioethics, and science-communication.

Synthetic biology also asks students to move beyond simply understanding or memorizing established scientific concepts. Students must integrate knowledge across disciplines, identify problems, develop possible solutions, and consider how biological systems could be engineered to address those problems.

For students with limited prior exposure to the field, this transition from learning scientific concepts to using those concepts to design biological solutions can be daunting without appropriate scaffolding.

We therefore designed our education program around a simple principle:

# Our Teaching Philosophy

Our curriculum is grounded in two interconnected principles: **Universal Design for Learning (UDL)** and **STEAM**.

<br>
<br>

## Student Centered Learning Through UDL

Our workshops emphasize **student voice** and **active participation**.

Direct interaction with students allows us as educators to respond to questions, clarify misconceptions, and adapt explanations based on students’ prior knowledge. Rather than treating education as a one-way transfer of information, we create opportunities for students to participate in discussions, work through problems in a collaborative manner, and brainstorm their own ideas.

This approach is grounded in **Universal Design for Learning (UDL)**, a framework centered on providing multiple means of **engagement, representation, and action and expression**. [INSERT CAST UDL GUIDELINES CITATION]

We incorporated these principles throughout our curricula by offering written explanations, visual representations, hands-on activities, guided discussion, and opportunities for students to communicate their own ideas.

In this manner, students encounter and interact with synthetic biology through multiple means of exposure to the content, rather than relying on a single mode of didactic instruction.

<br>
<br>

## STEAM: Making Science Accessible and Engaging

Creativity is fundamental to scientific problem-solving. Synthetic biologists use creativity to identify problems, design possible solutions, and determine how biological systems could be engineered to achieve a desired outcome.

Our curricula reflect this creative process through an emphasis on **STEAM: Science, Technology, Engineering, Art, and Mathematics**.

Art plays a particularly important role in our approach. Scientific illustrations, diagrams, comics, and other forms of visual storytelling can provide students multiple means of interacting with scientific ideas. In this way, we align the medium of STEAM with the pedagogical goals of UDL.

We therefore incorporated visual communication throughout our curricula. We used intentionally designed illustrations to explain concepts, tell scientific stories, guide activities, and help students visualize abstract processes that cannot easily be directly observed. All of our educational materials are digitally illustrated by hand.

By combining technical explanations with creative and visual activities, we made complex concepts more approachable while maintaining scientific rigor.

STEAM is the means through which we aim to create student-centered learning experiences. These experiences are mapped out into concrete points of evaluation in order to engage students through each step of the learning process.

## Measuring Success

Our three workshops were held in June, July, and October respectively. In the months between each workshop, we reflected on what went well and how we could improve upon our curriculum. In alignment with our guiding question, it was equally important to tailor each rendition of our workshop to our specific audiences. Because of the iterative nature of these workshops, it was crucial to establish a standardized means of measuring success.

# Designing Education through DBTL

We treated curriculum development as an iterative design process, using the DBTL cycle to guide each workshop iteration.

In synthetic biology, DBTL allows researchers to systematically design, build, test, and refine biological systems. We applied this same iterative mindset to our educational programs: each workshop gave us an opportunity to test our assumptions about what students needed, learn from their responses, and use those observations to design the next iteration.

{% include figure.html
    url="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6041/wiki/pages/education/dbtl-adapted.avif"
    caption="Education Figure 3: DBTL Cycle Adapted for Education"
    alttext="DBTL Cycle Adapted for Education"
    label="edu_dbtl_adapted"
%}

This framework enabled us to improve our curriculum based on both direct student feedback as post-workshop forms as well as indirect student feedback as observed engagement, understanding, and creativity.

Our events were also structured around a workshop model consisting of three main components; a mini lesson that introduces a concept through direct instruction, discussion, or an open-ended activity; a workshop time that gives students an opportunity to practice or apply the concept independently or in small groups; and a debrief time for reflecting on what students observed and learned. [CITE WORKSHOP MODEL]

This structure allowed us to combine direct instruction with active learning while providing students with opportunities to practice new concepts before applying them to increasingly complex scenarios.

## Design

Our first workshop was designed for high school students with varying levels of science background knowledge and diverse career interests.

Because students in this audience were more likely to have different levels of prior knowledge, we prioritized three goals:

- Introduce synthetic biology
- Establish relevance through real-world examples
- Give students an initial experience with scientific problem-solving

To create an engaging experience for our audience, we designed the workshop around three guiding questions:

- What is synthetic biology?
- Why do synthetic biologists work on real-world problems?
- What does synthetic biology look like in the laboratory?

Interactive questions were incorporated throughout the lesson to encourage students to connect new ideas to their own experiences and interests.

To provide students multiple means of engaging with the information in our slides, we developed a worksheet with illustrations and guiding questions to review the central dogma, learn parts of a plasmid, and provide students a take-home set of questions to be able to develop their own synthetic biology research project.

## Learn

**Accessibility begins with understanding what students already know and establishing a shared understanding of why the subject matters to us.**

Through our observations and conversations with students and camp staff after the workshop, we identified several areas for improvement.

**Establish Prior Knowledge:** Students needed additional context regarding foundational biological concepts before learning about how to engineer these existing biological tools.

**Increase Real-World Relevance:** Additional examples would help students understand why synthetic biology matters beyond the classroom.

**Improve Transitions:** Shorter transitions between activities would reduce cognitive load and help maintain continuity throughout the workshop.

**Create More Opportunities for Interaction:** More frequent opportunities for students to discuss ideas with one another and with educators would support engagement.

These observations directly informed our second iteration. We redesigned the curriculum to establish foundational knowledge more deliberately, emphasize relevance, and provide a more structured sequence of active learning experiences.

## Design

Our second workshop was designed for high school students with minimal prior exposure to synthetic biology but a more specific interest in science.

This audience allowed us to increase the technical depth of the curriculum while retaining the accessibility principles developed during Workshop 1.

We therefore focused on **scaffolding:** carefully sequencing concepts so that each new idea builds on knowledge that students have already encountered. We continued to structure the learning experience around the Reflective Theory of Change (Education Fig. 1). We designed our activity as a workbook with the intention of creating engaging learning experiences for students.

Students first encountered a concept through an activity or example, reflected on what they observed, connected those observations to scientific knowledge, and then applied that knowledge to a new situation.

We retained the workshop model but became more deliberate about the order in which information was introduced, working to effectively scaffold knowledge. Our goal was to move students progressively from foundational biology toward synthetic biology tools and, ultimately, experimental design.

<br>

**Understand why synthetic biology matters:** We introduced synthetic biology through real-world problems and examples relevant to our student audience.

**Explain the Central Dogma of molecular biology:** We illustrated a comic to introduce the central dogma and establish foundational knowledge before introducing synthetic biology tools.

**Apply synthetic biology tools to real-life scenarios:** Students were introduced to CRISPR and plasmids, and then given two potential scenarios to match which tool could be used to solve which problem.

**Evaluate the logic of synthetic genetic circuits:** Students explored genetic circuits and how multiple biological components can be designed to produce a desired outcome.

**Design a bioengineered solution to a real-world problem:** Students applied what they’d learned through a hands-on experimental design activity.

## Learn

**Students with limited prior exposure to synthetic biology can engage with technical concepts when those concepts are introduced through deliberate scaffolding and active participation.**

This sequence of introducing information beginning with foundational biology, bridging into synthetic biology tools, which leads into genetic circuits, which provides a limited toolbox of knowledge to begin thinking about experimental design, gave students a pathway for moving from simply understanding biological systems toward thinking like engineers.

Through this workshop, we saw the value of providing multiple ways for students to engage with the same concepts. The comic, workbook, discussion, and hands-on activities allow students to encounter and apply information through different forms of representation and expression, which aligns with our UDL framework.

## Design

Our third workshop was designed for first- and second-year undergraduate students studying a science discipline.

As with every iteration, we began by asking the practical application of our guiding question.

Unlike our previous high school audiences, these students were expected to have greater familiarity with scientific vocabulary.

Our audience of science undergraduates was expected to have greater familiarity with foundational biology. Because of this, rather than spending as much time introducing the central dogma as we had done during the previous workshop, we now had the opportunity to shift the emphasis towards understanding synthetic biology in a comprehensive sense. Because of this, we were able to include the ethics of experimental design as well as how art can be used to communicate the science that our students have learned. We accomplished this by incorporating lecture and activity segments on bioethics and science communication.

This shift in content allowed us to explore how the scaffolding that we developed through our first two workshops could be adapted for an audience of undergraduate students studying a science discipline.

## Learn

**We can tailor our curriculum to undergraduate students with greater prior knowledge by exploring the interdisciplinary nature of synthetic biology.**

Through this workshop, we learned the importance of meeting students at their knowledge levels. A post-workshop feedback survey revealed that eight out of fifteen responders had heard of synthetic biology before coming to the workshop. When asked to indicate their favorite sections, bioethics and science communication were the highest ranking sections. Students indicated that the illustrated workbook, real world examples, and small group discussions were the most effective learning tools that we implemented during the workshop.

The workbooks that were designed for our workshops are a culmination of months of iterations. Illustrations, diagrams, and science stories with sequential art are a representation of STEAM, while a focus on UDL through assessing knowledge with group discussions, drawings, and illustrated card games are the result of intentional curriculum design.

# What We Learned

Across our three iterative workshops, our curriculum evolved alongside our understanding of our student audience.

**Workshop 1: Intro to Synthetic Biology** taught us to **establish a foundation**. Students benefit from understanding both foundational biology as well as the motivation for why synthetic biology is relevant to them.

**Workshop 2: Engineering SynBio Solutions** taught us to **scaffold complexity**. Students can engage with increasingly technical synthetic biology concepts when information is presented deliberately and paired with opportunities to apply what they learned.

**Workshop 3: SynBio Solutions to Real-World Problems** allows us to **explore differentiation**. For students who already have experience studying science, we can shift the emphasis from introducing synthetic biology toward integrating knowledge and building depth alongside breadth of knowledge by including ethics and science-communication.

Our DBTL process therefore became more than just a means of structuring our education program. It became a way to meet our students at their knowledge level, tailoring our program to create an accessible and engaging experience of thinking like synthetic biologists. With each iteration of our curricula, direct and indirect student feedback shows an increase in our measures of success: student engagement, understanding, and creativity.

Through this framework we were able to continuously ask what our learners needed and use their responses to shape the next iteration, opening the door to a mutual conversation about student-centered learning.

# Our Vision

We envision a future where students from diverse backgrounds have opportunities to explore synthetic biology through curricula designed around their needs, perspectives, and experiences.

We hope to build learning communities where students feel empowered to ask questions, engage in scientific dialogue, and approach real-world problems with curiosity, confidence, and creativity.

Ultimately, our vision is not simply to teach students about synthetic biology.

It’s to give more students the opportunity to see themselves as people who can use it to create a positive impact by engineering synthetic biology solutions to real-world problems.

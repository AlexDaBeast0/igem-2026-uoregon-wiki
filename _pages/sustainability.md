Outline:

- What SDGs are and why they matter:
    - The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals for 2030 outline the 17 most important and distinct approaches towards which we may work together as a world to achieve “peace and prosperity for people and the planet” (United Nations, 2026). We feel that acknowledging and incorporating the appropriate SDGs into our project is not only our duty as global citizens, but also a vital contribution as liesans of the scientific community. Only when every sector of global development and progress-including that pursued by the scientific community-works to consider and incorporate their respective and aligned SDGs thoughtfully into each project they approach, may we move towards building the world we all want to be a part of.
    - Our team brings a diverse array of values and perspectives to the table, which has allowed for the development of a project that we believe will have a profound impact, not only on our most immediate community in the Eugene-Springfield area of Oregon, but also in the wide-spread rural areas of the United States, and of the World. As a team, we believe that everyone should have access to education about the world that surrounds them, and the ways in which our actions impact the Earth and each other. We also believe that everyone deserves to experience the greatest expression of their own physical, mental, and social health, which relies heavily on access to clean water, sanitation, education, and the independent choice in the consumption of products, with the ability to choose local and support small economies.
    - Hence, we have chosen to emphasize four key UN Sustainable Development Goals:
        - #3 Good Health
        - #4 Quality Education
        - #6 Clean Water and Sanitation
        - #12 Responsible Consumption and Production
- How we’re doing it.
    - #3 Good Health:
        - We chose to prioritize this project because we know how intimately it impacts people in our community (Eugene-Springfield, Oregon) with the knowledge that it is also very present around the United States, and the world. In the US alone, ONE out of every FIVE private wells has levels of nitrate that are considered unsafe by the Environmental Protection Agency. Excess nitrates in drinking water can lead to a whole host of negative health outcomes, including acute abdominal cramping and vomiting, as well as respiratory distress and in infants, Blue Baby syndrome. In the long-term, there are also chronic risks associated with high nitrate consumption, including thyroid dysfunction and stomach and bladder cancers. There is very little testing for and lack of education about the impacts of high nitrate levels in the human body, and as a result, many of these symptoms receive no formal diagnosis. Furthermore, the communities most impacted by these high levels of nitrate tend to exist in rural areas, which have both historically and currently been left out of some of the most major water testing and healthcare revolutions we have seen in the US over the course of the past several decades. We want this project to bring good health back to the communities most impacted by high nitrates and also to use our platform to discuss the discrepancies between environmental health outcomes in underrepresented and rural areas in comparison to the larger cities which tend to recieve more support and attention.

    - #12 Responsible Consumption and Production
        - Ultimately, our goal with this project is to achieve a circular economic model in which the bacteria we harvest from the AquaFlock system, now rich in complex nitrate chains called Cyanophycin, can be directly applied to the farms that themselves need fertilizer to continue producing food. We know that as a result of modern agriculture, it would indeed be challenging to step fully away from the use of fertilizer itself, however, we want ot be able to offer a cheaper, safer, and more circular option than the modern-day large-scale ammonium-based fertilizers that are causing the exact problem we are attempting to remedy.

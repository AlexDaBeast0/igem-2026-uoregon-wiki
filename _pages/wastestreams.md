# Devising a Plan

Using all of our research, we came up with an overarching plan to figure out how to best help our local community. The steps outlined below describe our target waste streams and factors we took into account when choosing how to best apply our research to tackle this problem.

## Step 1: Start Small

We first decided to focus our efforts on remediating waste streams associated with well water for a variety of reasons, all informed by the layers of outreach, research, and internal discourse that took place during the ideation process. After learning just how many people in our Eugene-Springfield community in Oregon, along with the broader United States, experience negative health outcomes as a result of high nitrate levels in well water, it became clear to our team just how vital it would be to find a way to remediate this issue. While working to address and educate about the upstream issues of nitrate pollution remains a broad goal for our team, we felt that taking our solution as close to the home as possible would have the most immediate human impact and allow us to continue to build out into wider environmental and agricultural remediation.

Furthermore, the particular waste stream, well water is significantly more accessible than some of the other potential waste streams we considered for this project. While rivers, agricultural runoff, and even lakes can experience extreme temperature fluctuations, highly variable organic and non-organic water composition and depositions, and challenging flow rates, well water tends towards relative consistency in composition, speed, and temperature throughout the year, making it much easier to design a biological product with certain demands for homeostasis.

## Step 2: Head Upstream

As we worked to develop a functional unit for implementation in the well water waste stream, we also set out to expand our impact in the direction of the source: agriculture.

In many rural areas in Oregon, much of the well water comes directly from groundwater that is fed by agricultural runoff. This often heavily fertilized land, when watered and rained upon, becomes that which is what is responsible for the high levels of nitrate we are trying to regulate. While this waste stream—agricultural runoff via subsurface drainage (see fig. 2)—is much more variable than well water (as outlined above), it still stands as a promising point of implementation, potentially allowing us to nip the bud at the source, and maybe even reduce the reliance on our product for well water remediation in the future.

## Step 3: Make it Big

We wanted to make a product that would last, be functional in a variety of waste streams, and have applications outside of simply pulling down nitrate concentrations. Nitrogen pollution is one of a variety of environmental pollutants with negative consequences. Our hope is that our strategy can be adapted to manage other chemicals that cause eutrophication to reduce pollution on our environment.

# Comparative Analysis & Key Takeaways

One of the most major alternative (and current) solutions related to our project is known as a wood chip bioreactor. Wood chip bioreactors, at their core, are essentially dug trenches that harness the power of carbon (from wood chips) to feed microorganisms from the soil that work to denitrify the water that flows out of the agricultural runoff and into the bioreactor. Our model recognizes many of the key mechanisms in this structure, including the implementation of bacteria, the use of carbon within the system, and an anaerobic environment. Where our product differs is in the control of water flow, the lessened amount of added carbon, the enhancement of the microbial system with our AquaFlock bacteria, and the ability to harvest said nitrogen-rich bacteria from the system itself. Similarly, the point of contact our system has with the waste stream is twofold: one near the home as part of the well water purification chain, and one upstream directly near subsurface drainage release from agricultural runoff.

We also performed a cost-benefit analysis on the most relevant at-home solutions for nitrate reduction currently on the market. As it stands, most, if not all, systems were expensive, required significant energetic input, or had drawbacks and/or limits in efficacy. Our proposed solution works to cut costs while providing the results that will keep people and the planet safe and healthy year over year.

# Important Considerations

Utilizing a bioreactor as a solution means that specific conditions need to be met in order for our bacteria to survive and thrive. PH levels, temperature control, oxygen availability, and flow rate are just some of the variables that can be adjusted within this system to make our bacteria as effective and efficient as possible. The goal is to have our system be implemented in well water systems and agricultural waste streams. This means that it is also important to consider the potential dangerous impact that microbial contamination, mechanical issues, and metabolic instability could have on water quality and the environment.
